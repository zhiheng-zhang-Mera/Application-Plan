# Statement of Objectives — SUTD PhD (Jan 2027)

**Draft status: NOT SUBMITTED**  
**Purpose:** programme-level statement for the standard SUTD PhD application; supervisor pre-approval is not assumed or required.

I want to pursue doctoral research on reliable, ubiquitous AI systems: software systems in which intelligent agents do not live inside a single application, but operate across multiple personal and edge devices while preserving user control, recoverability, and evidence about what actually happened.

My background has led me to this problem from the systems side. I completed a Bachelor of Science in Computer Science at the University of British Columbia and am completing a research Master's degree in Computer Science at the University of Melbourne. Across coursework and independent research engineering, I have become most effective when a research question can be made concrete as a working system, exercised under realistic conditions, and then challenged through failure analysis and controlled experiments. I am less interested in treating an AI model as an isolated object than in understanding the software and infrastructure required for that model to act reliably over time.

The central project behind my current research direction is Utopia/Digital City, a multi-device personal-computing platform. Its current accepted product boundary includes Android and Web control surfaces, authenticated device/control paths, and a three-end mesh in which two real Windows workers and an Android control surface operate one canonical environment with strict target-device routing and cross-host formal review. This matters to me because it changes the research question from “can an assistant call a tool?” to “how should a persistent computing environment decide which device or capability should execute an action, how should that decision survive failures, and how can the user verify the result?”

Utopia has also become a testbed for a second question: how should a system preserve research evidence while it evolves? The associated research-strengthening programme has accepted stages for experiment registration, trace/provenance capture, repeated controlled scenarios, fault injection, and replay/ablation. Some later stages, such as artifact export and the final reproducibility freeze, were still under independent review or dependency gating at my current application snapshot. I consider that distinction important. I want my doctoral work to treat incomplete evidence and failed tests as research data rather than hide them behind a polished product narrative.

My next planned direction is a Personal Compute Fabric: a heterogeneous runtime that can observe the capabilities and current state of nearby devices, place work across them, recover from partial failures, and expose why a placement or fallback occurred. This is not a feature I claim to have completed. It is the research problem I want to study. A personal AI system may have access to a workstation, a phone, a wearable device, or future embedded endpoints, but availability and capability will change continuously. A useful runtime must decide where work should run without becoming an opaque scheduler. It should be possible to measure the decision, reproduce it, and understand the effect of a device or network failure.

Three research questions are especially important to me.

First, how should heterogeneous-device context influence placement and execution? Traditional distributed systems often treat nodes as resources with known capabilities. Personal devices are more irregular: they differ in hardware, power, connectivity, sensors, foreground use, privacy constraints, and user proximity. I would like to study scheduling and offloading policies that combine hard eligibility constraints with measurable contextual signals, while remaining stable enough to avoid unnecessary migration.

Second, how should long-running agent tasks recover? My DS-Hns project approaches this problem from autonomous software engineering. It supports persistent task state, restart/recovery-oriented execution, and explicit terminal-state evidence for long-running repository work. This project taught me that process survival is not equivalent to task completion. A restarted agent can resume execution while losing the semantic reason behind previous steps, duplicating work, or reaching a terminal condition that is technically satisfied but substantively weak. For doctoral research, I want to study recovery as a correctness problem: what state and evidence must survive, which invariants should be re-established, and how can alternative recovery strategies be evaluated under controlled interruption?

Third, how should users and evaluators retain meaningful control over increasingly autonomous systems? My Codex Boss project explores evidence-based acceptance and multi-agent critique/adjudication. The larger lesson is that autonomy is only useful when a system can expose the provenance of its actions and the limits of its own claims. In a multi-device environment, this becomes more difficult because reasoning, execution, sensing, and confirmation may occur on different endpoints. I am interested in interfaces and control planes that provide progressive disclosure: ordinary users should not be overwhelmed by raw internal logs, but they should still be able to inspect important decisions, intervene when necessary, and distinguish graceful degradation from silent failure.

SUTD is attractive to me because these questions sit across conventional boundaries between software systems, AI, human-computer interaction, and device/edge computing. I am looking for a doctoral environment where building and evaluating a real system is an acceptable way to answer a research question, and where work can connect reliable AI to the engineering constraints of deployment. The current SUTD research landscape in trustworthy software/AI, reliable agent systems, and interactive intelligent systems makes it possible to refine my topic without forcing it into a purely theoretical or purely application-specific form.

I am particularly interested in using a PhD to convert my current engineering evidence into a disciplined experimental programme. A first stage would define a small number of representative multi-device workloads and a machine-readable experiment model. The system would record device capability, placement decisions, retries, failures, user interventions, and final outcomes. Repetition would make it possible to distinguish a robust mechanism from a successful demonstration.

A second stage would introduce controlled perturbations: device loss, stale identity, delayed network paths, resource pressure, duplicated commands, or provider failures. Rather than asking only whether the system eventually finishes, I would measure recovery time, task correctness, placement stability, intervention cost, and whether the provenance remains sufficient to reconstruct what occurred.

A third stage would compare policies through replay and ablation. For example, a placement policy could be evaluated with and without context-aware routing, or a recovery mechanism could be disabled to quantify what it actually contributes. If a learned or LLM-assisted component is introduced, it should be bounded by systems constraints and compared against simpler deterministic baselines. I want to avoid claiming that an intelligent policy is better merely because it is more complex.

I also see value in vertical applications, but I do not want them to replace the core systems question. Wearable interfaces, a personal health terminal, or an entertainment/creative environment could provide demanding workloads for the same personal compute fabric. A wearable scenario, for example, introduces sensing, mobility, battery limits, and changing connectivity. A health scenario adds stricter provenance and user-confirmation requirements. These domains are useful because they stress the runtime differently, not because I intend to make my PhD primarily about medicine or wearable hardware.

My prior work has also shaped how I want to conduct research. I prefer to preserve negative results and implementation failures when they reveal a general mechanism. In recent Utopia research work, controlled probes exposed cases where apparently successful components could still leave incorrect evidence, unstable storage behavior, or a mismatch between a declared routing rule and the runtime path that was actually executed. The value of those failures was not simply that they were fixed; they produced reusable test cases and clarified what the system needed to measure. I want to carry this approach into doctoral research: design systems that can be challenged, keep the failure trail, and build claims that an independent reviewer can reproduce.

I would bring to SUTD substantial experience in iterative system building, Git/GitHub-based experimental development, regression testing, cross-host review, and working with multiple AI systems as engineering collaborators while retaining independent verification. I am comfortable constructing a software artifact, identifying where its evidence is weak, and redesigning the measurement around that weakness. I also understand that a PhD requires more than building a large platform. My goal is to extract a small number of falsifiable questions from the platform and evaluate them with controlled methodology.

By the end of the PhD, I would like the research to contribute both mechanisms and evidence: a reusable personal/edge execution fabric for agent workloads; concrete scheduling and recovery policies; a trace and provenance model that supports reproducible evaluation; and empirical results that show when those mechanisms help, when they fail, and what forms of user intervention remain necessary.

My longer-term goal is to work on intelligent computing systems that remain useful beyond a single device or application. I see future personal AI not as one model in one interface, but as a coordinated environment spanning devices, services, and human decisions. The research challenge is to make that environment reliable enough to trust, transparent enough to control, and measurable enough to study scientifically. SUTD's combination of computing, systems thinking, and design-oriented research makes it a strong place for me to develop that agenda.
