# Statement of Objectives — SUTD PhD, January 2027

**English candidate; NOT SUBMITTED; for the standard SUTD PhD programme rather than any specific Fall 2027 supervisor opening.**

My doctoral research goal is to make long-running personal AI agents reliable enough to accomplish real user tasks across the devices people already use. I am particularly interested in wearable and ubiquitous computing as an environment for systems research: lightweight devices provide sensing and interaction, while personal servers and nearby computers provide controlled execution resources. The central question is not merely whether an agent can invoke a tool. It is whether the agent can preserve an authorized goal, adapt to unexpected failures, and provide real evidence that the user’s intended outcome has been achieved.

I completed a Bachelor of Science in Computer Science at the University of British Columbia and am completing a Master of Computer Science research project at the University of Melbourne. My research engineering background has led me toward systems questions that can be tested in running software: reliability under interruption, persistent execution state, traceable evidence, and the gap between passing a technical check and completing a meaningful task. I would like to use a PhD to turn these practical observations into falsifiable research hypotheses and reproducible experiments.

One foundation is Utopia/Digital City, a personal-computing software project with Android and Web control surfaces and a bounded three-end environment spanning two real Windows execution nodes and an Android controller. Its Personal Compute Fabric line explores typed resource observation, executable workload contracts, bounded task dispatch and result integrity. Work in the research-evaluation line provides fault injection, replay, provenance and reproducibility records. The accepted evidence includes real cross-host execution and independently checked experimental material. I am careful not to extend those results beyond the tested boundary: Utopia is not yet a production-ready wearable assistant, the planned dual-server high-availability deployment remains future work, and physical validation across all proposed device types is not complete.

Two related projects examine persistent agent behaviour more directly. DS-Hns explores long-running repository work with durable task lifecycles and crash/resume-oriented recovery. Codex Boss investigates orchestrated agents, review decisions and evidence-based completion claims. A repeated lesson from these projects is that a surviving process, a successful automated test and a completed user objective are different things. An agent may resume after a restart but lose the semantic reason behind previous steps, or report success after a local repair that invalidates another completed component. Such failures provide a more useful research starting point than counting lines of code or the number of integrated modules.

I would pursue three linked research questions at SUTD.

First, **how can a long-horizon agent remain faithful to the user’s authorized objective?** A reliable assistant should clarify the desired outcome and any essential constraints before starting autonomous work. It should record what actions are permitted, which conditions indicate completion, and what requires further human authorization. This goal contract could be checked during execution to detect premature commitment, state drift and unsupported claims of completion. The intended benefit is reduced supervision without reducing the user’s ability to understand and redirect the system.

Second, **how can an agent revise a failing plan without compromising earlier work or future dependencies?** I would study bounded replanning mechanisms that assess global impact before changing an implementation route. They would preserve accepted task state and dependency links, reject changes that exceed authorization or spending limits, and look for feasible alternatives if the user declines an additional permission request. A personal computing hub with a standby server offers a concrete environment to test recovery, interrupted connectivity and consistency, while mobile and wearable interfaces can retain limited local functions during an outage.

Third, **what counts as successful completion in a real personal AI system?** I would develop controlled workloads, fault-injection scenarios and outcome-based validation methods that separate the agent’s narration from independently observed results. Comparison with deterministic and conventional tool-using agent baselines would measure real task success, false-completion reports, unnecessary human interruptions, recovery correctness and end-to-end response time. I would use replay and ablation to isolate which parts of a proposed mechanism genuinely contribute to reliability. Whenever usability depends on physical conditions or different user needs that simulations cannot represent faithfully, I would use appropriately reviewed real-device or user studies and report untested cases as untested.

My intended application setting is software-first personal AI. An intelligent wearable should be comfortable and lightweight; it need not perform demanding model inference itself if a nearby trusted personal computer can do the work. The normal execution centre would be a configured server, with an independently monitored standby server for recovery. Other everyday PCs should be enlisted only under explicit user authorization, rather than silently consumed as a distributed resource pool. Wearables, phones and a future smart room would primarily provide context and interaction, with limited local computation for degraded or offline use. I would use existing devices wherever possible, building simple auxiliary prototypes only if necessary to answer a research question; I do not plan to make circuit design or robotics control the centre of the doctorate.

A further supporting question is how a personal agent can use long-term knowledge responsibly. I envision contextual observations being distilled into candidate knowledge, organized by domain and authority, and periodically reviewed by users. Preferences explicitly declared by the user would remain authoritative. Agent-driven software improvements could be researched and tested autonomously, but deployment to the user’s active environment would require approval and an understandable rollback path. These are potential directions for controlled experiments, not claims that the current platform has already realized them.

SUTD’s design-oriented and interdisciplinary environment is attractive because these research questions span trustworthy agents, software systems, human interaction and practical edge computing. I am interested in joining a supervisor’s ongoing research and would adapt my experimental platform to its methods and facilities. Celestial is a future personal research-workspace concept that could help manage multi-domain experiments and knowledge, but I do not regard preservation of its architecture as a prerequisite for doctoral work. The scholarly value should come from the mechanisms, controls and evidence, not from insisting on a particular software brand or codebase.

I would expect an early stage of the PhD to identify representative long-horizon tasks, formalize authorization and outcome criteria, and build transparent baselines. A subsequent stage would implement global-impact-aware replanning and failure recovery, testing it under changing device and tool availability. A later stage would investigate real-device tasks and, where appropriate, user studies to determine whether the mechanisms deliver more useful results with less unnecessary supervision. Each stage would retain failures, excluded cases and evidence limitations so that claimed improvements could be checked independently.

My longer-term goal is to contribute to personal AI systems that remain useful beyond a single application or device. The scientific challenge is to make sustained autonomy reliable and inspectable without turning users into full-time operators. I hope to develop systems techniques that genuinely improve task outcomes, carefully document when they fail, and establish empirical methods for evaluating everyday agent reliability.
